Panels, Cabinets and Pitch

An LED wall is a grid of identical cabinets, each one a dumb block of LEDs plus a small computer. Almost every problem you'll troubleshoot is one panel, one data cable, or one power cable — and the pattern of what's dark tells you which.

Read the failure to find the fault. A black rectangle inside a panel = module. A whole black panel = that panel's card or power. A black panel and everything after it in the run = the data chain broke at that panel.

Pixel pitch — the number that decides everything

What it means

Pitch is the distance in millimetres between the centres of adjacent LEDs. P3.91 means 3.91 mm apart. Smaller number = tighter pixels = finer image = more money.

Pitch also sets resolution per panel. A 500 × 500 mm cabinet at P3.91 is 128 × 128 pixels. The same cabinet at P2.6 is 192 × 192. Same physical size, more than double the pixel count — which is why pitch drives your processor and port capacity, not just the picture.

The viewing-distance rule of thumb

Minimum comfortable viewing distance in metres ≈ the pitch number. P3.91 looks clean from about 4 m back. P2.6 from about 2.6 m. Closer than that and the audience starts seeing individual pixels.

So the real question is never "what's the best pitch" — it's "how close is the nearest person?" A stage backdrop 10 m from the front row is fine at P4.8 and wasted money at P1.9. A lobby wall people walk up to needs the tight pitch.

Cabinet formats

The common sizes

500 × 500 mm — the rental standard. Square, stacks and hangs in any arrangement, easy maths.

500 × 1000 mm — half as many cabinets for the same area, faster to build, heavier per piece.

600 × 337.5 mm and similar 16:9 formats — sized so a whole wall lands on a native 16:9 aspect without maths.

Know your cabinet size before you plan anything. Wall dimensions are always a whole number of cabinets — you cannot have half a panel.

Front service vs. rear service

Rear service — modules and cards come out from behind. Standard, cheaper, but you need working space behind the wall.

Front service — everything pulls from the face with a magnet tool. Essential when the wall is against a hard wall, ground-stacked tight, or built into scenery.

This is a question you ask before the truck is loaded. Rear-service panels flat against a ballroom wall means you cannot fix a dead module without dismantling the wall.

Types you'll meet

  • Standard rental — the workhorse. Fast-lock latches, hangs or stacks, 500 × 500.
  • Carbon fibre / tour — the same thing much lighter. Matters when you're flying a lot of it or trucking daily.
  • Flex / curve — panels that bend, with adjustable angle locks for convex and concave. Columns, cylinders, wave walls.
  • Transparent / mesh — high see-through percentage. Goes over glass or in front of a set so you can light through it.
  • Floor / dance floor — load-rated to be walked on, with an anti-slip and impact-resistant face. Ask the actual rating before anyone stands on it.
  • Poster / all-in-one — self-contained standing displays for lobbies and wayfinding. Usually just needs power and a media source.
  • Stadium / ball-hit — reinforced face for sports. Weatherproofed.

If there is a camera in the room, refresh rate is not optional. A low-refresh panel produces scan lines or banding on camera even though it looks perfect to the eye — the camera's shutter and the panel's refresh beat against each other. 3840 Hz is the practical floor for anything being shot; 5760 Hz is safer. Ask before you accept the gear, because you cannot fix it on the day.

Data and Power — how a wall actually gets fed

That last line is the single most useful diagnostic on a wall. If one panel and every panel after it went black, it's data. If a block of panels went black but the chain continues past them, it's power.

The processor

What it does

Takes a normal video signal and turns it into what LED panels understand. Four jobs:

1. Scale — fits your source resolution to the wall's actual pixel dimensions, which are almost never a standard resolution.
2. Map — tells each panel which part of the image it's showing, based on how you physically cabled them.
3. Process — brightness, gamma, colour temperature, so the wall matches the room and the cameras.
4. Drive — outputs over Cat6 to the panels.

The map is the part that must match reality. The processor doesn't know how you cabled the wall — you tell it, and if what you tell it differs from what you built, the image comes out scrambled, mirrored or offset.

Port capacity — the limit that decides your cabling

Each processor output port carries a fixed number of pixels — commonly around 650,000 per gigabit port, though it varies by manufacturer and you must check your processor.

The maths: panel pixel count × panels on that port must stay under the port limit.

A P3.91 500 mm cabinet is 128 × 128 = 16,384 pixels. At a 650,000 limit, that's about 39 panels per port in theory. In practice you plan well under it, because you also want the runs physically sensible and you want headroom.

Exceed the port limit and the panels past the limit go black — and it looks exactly like a broken cable, which is why people chase the wrong fault for an hour.

The power-run limit is not advisory. Manufacturers publish a maximum number of panels per power daisy chain — often somewhere around 6 to 10 depending on panel and voltage. That figure exists because the link-through connector and the conductor inside are rated for a specific current.

Exceed it and you're overloading a connector, not just tripping a breaker. Count the panels on every power run, every time.

Redundancy

The backup loop

Most processors have primary and backup ports. You run data out of the primary into the first panel, chain through, and bring the last panel's output back to the backup port.

What it buys you: if the data chain breaks in the middle, the panels downstream of the break get fed backwards from the other end. Instead of losing half the wall, you lose one panel.

It costs one extra cable run per port. On any show that matters, run it.

Build order

  1. Confirm the wall dimensions in panels, not feet. Cabinets × cabinets, and check it against the drawing.
  2. Rig or stack the structure — hanging bars and bumpers, or ground-stack base. Rigging a wall is rigging: certified hardware, rated points, and the right people.
  3. Hang panels bottom-up (stacked) or top-down (flown), working consistently so latches align.
  4. Power runs first. Count panels per run against the limit. Label each run at the distro.
  5. Data runs. Serpentine within each port's block, keep the physical path matching what you'll tell the processor.
  6. Backup loop from the last panel of each chain back to the processor.
  7. Power up and check for a full white field — every panel lit, no dead modules, no colour shift between cabinets.
  8. Map in the processor to match how you actually cabled it. Send a test pattern with numbers so you can see any panel that's in the wrong place.
  9. Set brightness and colour temp to the room, and verify on camera if cameras are present.
  10. Note the map and the runs on paper before you walk away.
